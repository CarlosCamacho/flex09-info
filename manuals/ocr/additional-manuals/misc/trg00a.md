USING THIS MANUAL

 Each chapter of this manual is designed to take you a step  farther  along  with
 the  capabilities  of  SCREDITOR III. Even though you may be quite familiar with
 the material presented  in  a  given  chapter,  you  should  still  perform  the
 exercises  in that chapter since references will often be made later to work you
 have done.

 The first page of each chapter will include a  brief  description  of  what  the
 chapter will accomplish, what you will learn, and major ideas to be presented in
 the remainder of the chapter.

 At the end of each chapter there will be a short summary  of  the  major  points
 presented in the chapter as a refresher.

 Throughout  this  manual,  COMMANDS  (which are entered in COMMAND MODE) will be
 shown either by their full name (such as LOG or ABORT) or  by  their  two-letter
 abbreviation  (such  as  LO  or  AB).  OPERATORS (which are executed by typing a
 single key) will be shown either by their full name (such as CURSOR LEFT or  TAB
 RIGHT)  or by their two-letter abbreviation preceded by an up-arrow (such as ^CL
 or ^TR).


WHAT IS SCREDITOR III?

 How  does  SCREDITOR  III  differ  from  other  editors,  text  processors, word
 processors, etc...? First, let's define what these other programs are.  Then  we
 will  see  how  SCREDITOR III is different. In reading this section, by the way,
 you may encounter words with which you are not familiar. Don't worry about them,
 as  they  will  be  explained  later.  Just  read  to get a general feel for the
 material.


FIRST, THERE WERE EDITORS

 In the beginning of computing, there was a need to enter text into  a  computer.
 The Text Editor, or simply "editor" program was written to fill this need.

 An  editor  is  a program which allows you to create and change a document. With
 most older editors, you were limited to simply typing the  material  with  which
 you  were  working.  Generally,  to  make  corrections, additions and deletions,
 special commands had to be given to the computer to perform the operation. After
 this,  you would have had to request that the computer display the result of the
 command you just entered. It was like making corrections at a typewriter without
 being  able to look at the paper until after you make the change! At first, this
 wasn't much of a problem because programmers are funny people anyway,  and  they
 were the only ones using editors!

 Even  programmers  occasionally write things for real people to read. Of course,
 they used the same editors that they used for programming. With  these  editors,
 though,  document  formatting  such  as justification, centering, tabular entry,
 etc..., had to be done manually as text was entered, again, without knowing what
 the document would look like until after it was typed. Therefore, with an editor
 program, you didn't generally have any "feel" for how your final document  would
 look.


THEN CAME TEXT PROCESSORS

 Since early editors were impractical for producing  a  final  printed  document,
 smart  programmers  took  the  next step. They wrote programs which could read a
 file created by an editor, and on the basis of special  commands  in  the  file,
 create the printed document. These programs were called Text Processors.

 With a text processor, you still used an editor, but a number of lines  of  your
 text would consist of commands which would later tell the text processor program
 how to format the resulting document. For instance, if a  paragraph  was  to  be
 indented,  you  might  have  had to type .PI5 on the line immediately before the
 paragraph to be so indented. To all-justify the paragraph, you would have had to
 add  another  line  such  as .JU, and to indent the first line of the paragraph,
 another line still would be added such as .IN5.  After  the  paragraph,  similar
 commands  had  to be entered to turn off justification and indentation. In other
 words, your edited, but unprocessed, document might look like this...

 .PI 5
 .JU
 .IN 5
 This is a sample of what your text might have
 looked  like to
 produce the following paragraph as it would be printed in
 the final document.
 .NJ
 .IN 0

 and the result after processing would look like this...


      This  is  a sample of what your text would have had to look like to produce
 the following paragraph as it would be printed in the final document.


 As  you  can  see,  these  commands  could  be very powerful, but added a lot of
 overhead to a document. In addition, it was often quite difficult  to  determine
 from  what  we  called  the source document (which was entered using the editor)
 what the final, or object document (produced by the processor) would look  like.
 Again,  this  seemed to be no real problem for programmers, who delight in doing
 things the hard way!


THEN CAME WORD PROCESSING

 Along the line, someone got the idea that a computer could make life easier  for
 clerical  workers,  much  of whose time was spent typing. The available software
 was examined to determine whether it would fill their need.

 As can be seen, editors and text processors did not provide a convenient way  to
 create  and  modify  a document. Imbedded commands made it hard to tell just how
 the final document would look. Quite  often,  repeated  print-outs  and  re-edit
 operations  had  to  be performed to make the result of the imbedded commands do
 just what was desired.

 As a result, Word Processing programs such as SCREDITOR III were written.  These
 programs    generally    incorporate   a   different   philosophy   than   older
 editors...mainly, that real people would be using them!

 First, editing is done on the screen exactly as if the  screen  were  the  final
 paper  document  itself.  Unlike paper, however, correcting mistakes is simply a
 matter of moving the cursor to the spot to be changed  and  making  the  change.
 Mis-spellings,  for  instance,  only  require  that you "overtype" the incorrect
 letters with the proper ones. Moving, adding or deleting words, sentences,  even
 paragraphs,  is  a simple process, and the results are immediately visible. With
 this type of "screen" editing,  you  have  complete  visual  control  over  your
 document preparation.

 In  addition,  better  word  processors  allow you to automatically justify your
 document in a number of ways.  The  result  of  justification,  as  with  making
 corrections,  is immediately visible on the screen. Unlike typewriters, however,
 there is no counting and spacing to be done. You simply tell the word  processor
 how  you  want  a paragraph justified, and it does it for you! At all times, you
 know what your document will look like.

 Since most displays will not show you an entire printed page  at  a  time,  many
 modern word processors such as SCREDITOR III are called "window" processors. The
 effect is as if you were looking through a small window  at  your  document,  as
 shown  by  FIGURE  ONE  which  folds  out from the back of this manual. Changes,
 additions, deletions, etc..., are made in the window. Any time you wish to  look
 at  another part of your document, commands are available to "move" the document
 up, down, left or right "behind" the window. The next part of this  manual  will
 discuss  the  screen  that you will be looking at with SCREDITOR III, one of the
 better word processors available today!
